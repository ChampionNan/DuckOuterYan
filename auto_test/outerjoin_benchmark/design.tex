\subsection{Outer-Join Benchmark}
\label{sec:outerjoin-benchmark}

Standard analytical benchmarks do not isolate the factors that make outer-join
optimization difficult. TPC-H~\cite{tpch}, TPC-DS~\cite{tpcds}, JOB~\cite{job},
and SQLStorm~\cite{sqlstorm25} provide realistic schemas and join relationships,
but they do not systematically vary preservation semantics and join-tree shape.
We therefore use their queries as sources of realistic \emph{join skeletons},
rather than treating an inner-join query and its outer-join rewrite as
semantically equivalent workloads.

The core benchmark contains 34 traceable seeds: 5 from TPC-H, 7 from TPC-DS,
10 from JOB, and 12 from SQLStorm. From each seed, we extract a connected,
acyclic equality-join skeleton containing 3--6 relation instances. We retain
the relation graph, normalize every active edge to one plain column equality,
assign deterministic mixed INNER/LEFT/RIGHT/FULL labels to each structure. Each generated selection remains in the global \texttt{WHERE}
clause and is NULL-rejecting. Unary \texttt{ON} conjuncts and equality-side
casts are excluded because they are outside OuterYan's current applicability
envelope. The mixed labels exercise preservation and filter pushdown; their physical
realization is determined by optimization.

Each skeleton produces five deterministic applicability-oriented groupings:
primary left- and right-deep trees (\texttt{ldmx}/\texttt{rdmx}); a balanced
bushy tree when the graph permits one, otherwise an applicability-checked mixed
left-deep traversal (\texttt{bmx}/\texttt{alt}); an additional connected bushy
tree, or a secondary right-deep tree when a bushy root is structurally
unavailable (\texttt{bdmx}/\texttt{rdax}); and an alternate right-deep traversal
that starts from the opposite graph side (\texttt{rduX}). This gives 170 queries
with 45 left-deep, 79 right-deep, and 46 genuinely bushy trees. Explicit
parentheses fix the binary join tree.

\paragraph{Semantic selectivity.}
We parameterize selection families from the source queries: reporting-date and
release-year intervals, inventory and episode-number ranges, product-color and
person-name patterns, and return-reason or information-type categories. We use
neither identifier-modulo nor hash sampling. Each variant replaces the complete
selection predicate, avoiding compounded selectivity from an additional filter
on an already narrow cohort. The three base-entity retention bands are
$(0,0.05)$, $[0.05,0.40)$, and $[0.40,0.80]$; no additional band above $0.80$
is generated. Source adaptations, including explicitly added business filters
for SQLStorm queries without an original selection, are recorded in the manifest.
For a business predicate $P$ over its declared population $R$, calibration uses
\[
  s(P;R)=\frac{|\{r\in R : P(r)\text{ is true}\}|}{|R|}.
\]
NULL-valued records remain in the denominator. Dimension attributes such as
sales date or return reason are calibrated over their fact populations, not
uniformly over dimension rows. These bands do not specify final join-result
cardinality. Literals are frozen using base-entity counts independently of
runtime and plan selection, and audited again when the dataset scale changes.
We merge the structural and selectivity expansions by normalized SQL identity,
including the execution family and preserving predicates, literal values, join
kinds and grouping. Comments and formatting do not distinguish queries. The
resulting corpus contains 548 non-aggregate queries (480 source-derived and 68
graph queries) and 102 aggregation queries, for 650 distinct SQL queries before
runtime and correctness exclusions. The 18-query selectivity-sensitivity study
references a subset of this corpus rather than adding repeated observations.
All source aliases remain in the provenance mapping, while query names and
reported categories describe query type, workload family and structural/filter
properties without suite-version labels.

This construction provides a controlled and representative measure of
outer-join performance. Holding the relation graph fixed while varying tree
grouping isolates enumeration behavior from unrelated query-content changes.
The left-deep, right-deep, and bushy trees span multiple source schemas, while
the applicability-oriented labels ensure that every generated case reaches
OuterYan rather than being discarded after pre-optimizer simplification. Projecting columns from
multiple inputs keeps NULL-extension observable whenever it survives the
declared NULL-rejecting selection.

We also include the legacy workload in
\path{auto_test/outerjoin_query_old/} as a separate industry-oriented test
corpus. Its 170 outer-join queries are derived from workload patterns observed
in DBMS-X and instantiated over public benchmark schemas, thereby simulating
real industrial use cases while remaining reproducible. The corpus covers
practical patterns such as optional dimension enrichment, sparse relationships,
fact-completeness checks, and preserved master data, complementing the
controlled variants in the core benchmark.

\paragraph{Compared configurations.}
The conservative cost-model configuration uses semi-join weight
$\lambda_{\mathrm{SJ}}=1.0$. We separately identify the paper-example
experimental configuration, which uses $\lambda_{\mathrm{SJ}}=0.20$ and
accepts a changed grouping when
\[
  C_{\mathrm{real\ join}} + 0.20\,C_{\mathrm{SJ}}
  \leq 1.20\,C_{\mathrm{DuckDB}},
\]
subject to the existing applicability, grouping, and uncertainty checks.
The reduced weight is an experimental parameter motivated by the example,
not a calibrated replacement for the original default. Results from the two
configurations must be labeled and reported separately. Optimizer-only
selection counts and counterfactual gate-threshold analyses do not constitute
measured runtime improvements or bounds on runtime slowdown.

\subsubsection{PostgreSQL Plan Comparison}
\label{sec:pgplan-comparison}

Building on the motivating example in the introduction, we use two
three-table queries derived from JOB~3c~\cite{job} and TPC-H~Q3~\cite{tpch}
to test whether OuterYan exposes useful plans beyond
PostgreSQL~16.2's search space. The first contains INNER and LEFT joins;
the second contains FULL joins. Both reuse the original benchmark tables.
We replay PostgreSQL's selected typed join tree in DuckDB
(\emph{PG-Replay}) and compare it with native \emph{DuckDB} and normally
selected \emph{OuterYan}, using the same engine, data, and execution settings.
\fix{All three modes produce the same complete result multiset as native
PostgreSQL.}
Table~\ref{tab:pgplan-runtime} reports runtime and actual join input.
SQL, plan diagrams, and measurement details are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:pgplan-queries}.

The core evidence is an explicit search-space restriction, established
through PostgreSQL's legality and enumeration traces~\cite{pg16-optimizer}.
\fix{For the JOB-derived case, PostgreSQL rejects joining \texttt{title}
with \texttt{movie\_info} before \texttt{movie\_keyword}, whereas OuterYan
selects this grouping. For the TPC-H-derived case, PostgreSQL retains the
FULL-join boundary around \texttt{lineitem} and \texttt{orders}, while
OuterYan joins \texttt{orders} with \texttt{customer} first.}
OuterYan's simplification, two-round reduction, and de-simplification
make these additional groupings available over transformed inputs.
\fix{Compared with PG-Replay, OuterYan reduces real-join input by
[A reduction]\% and [B reduction]\%, and achieves speedups of
[A speedup]$\times$ and [B speedup]$\times$, respectively.
These cases demonstrate that the expanded search space contains useful
equivalent plans that improve execution in the same engine.}
The measurements compare complete plans, including reduction and other
optimizer effects; completeness is a separate theoretical claim.
